[case study 03]

Fyzz

  • in progress

A trip planner for casual plans, whether that's a group, a date, or just you. Everyone votes on places, and Fyzz builds the day around the weather, opening hours and travel time.

role
Product designer
timeline
Aug 2026 – now
context
Personal project
status
In progress · not tested with groups yet

01 · problem + constraints

Not a crazy commitment unless it wants to be

Most casual plans happen in a group chat. Someone drops a few pins, the plan ends up spread across screenshots and old messages, and nobody checks whether it'll rain during the outdoor stop.

The tools that exist each cover one piece of it. Google Maps lists let you vote on places but don't schedule anything. Wanderlog schedules things. TripIt organizes bookings you've already made, but it doesn't help you decide anything.

Put those pieces together. Let people vote, build the day from the votes, check it against the weather and opening hours, and ask before changing a plan people already agreed to. The line I kept coming back to was "not a crazy commitment unless it wants to be."

my role
I did all the design work: the design doc, the survey, the mockups and the design system.
how I worked
Every decision went into a log with the situation, what I considered, what I picked, and what it cost. Most of this page comes straight from it.

Research

I didn't start with research. I wrote the design doc on September 4 and the build started the same day. About a week and a half in, I ran a survey to check my assumptions against how people actually plan, and it's been steering what Fyzz does since.

Out of 16 people I surveyed

12 like a rough plan with room to change things
13 had a recent outing that was busier than they expected
10 named someone flaking or running late, the most common friction
  • People want a loose plan. Only 2 of 16 like having everything planned out. That's the idea behind "Pack my day" and "Keep it loose," and why a plan can still bend on the day.
  • The scattered group chat wasn't the big problem. I'd assumed it was, but only 4 of 16 picked "information gets lost across texts and apps." Someone flaking or running late came up far more (10 of 16). That's what the trip-day rules are for: the itinerary stays open until the day's over, and anyone can change timing once you're out.
  • Crowds disrupt plans, but people don't check for them. 13 of 16 hit a place busier than they expected, yet only 7 look up how busy a place will be, probably because there's no good way to. When I went looking, I couldn't find a public API for wait times either, so Fyzz will have to collect them from the people using it.

"A really good plan that was flexible enough for spontaneity."

Survey answer to "What made the best outing you can remember actually good?"

16 responses, collected September 13–19, 2026, mostly from people I know, so I treat it as directional, not statistical. Eight people offered to chat more, and those follow-ups are next.

02 · key decisions

Three choices that shaped it

  1. 01

    Nobody needs an account

    options
    Require an account to join, or let anyone join from a link, a QR code or a short code.
    I chose
    No sign-up. Guests get an anonymous account behind the scenes, and their trip list lives on their device.
    why
    You shouldn't have to sign up just to find your way back to something you already joined.
    cost
    A guest who opens a private window is a new person. So when someone leaves, their votes go with them, and blocking someone who was removed isn't solved yet.

    It's the same call I made on Karla Learn and GATORE: don't make people commit before they see anything. Here it cost the most.

  2. 02

    Hearts and sign-off are different votes

    options
    Call both "voting," like the brief did, or give them different words.
    I chose
    Hearts for places, sign-off for the whole day. The app never calls sign-off a vote.
    why
    Hearts say how much a place matters to you, and they shape the draft. Sign-off is a yes on the whole day. Two meanings for one word would have confused people.

    People joining or leaving doesn't reset sign-off. The version didn't change, so starting over would just punish the people who already answered.

  3. 03

    If the data isn't there, it says so

    options
    Fill gaps with placeholder ratings, stock photos and blurbs, or show what's missing.
    I chose
    Real Google Places data where it exists, and a plain "no photo" or "coming soon" where it doesn't.
    why
    People can trust everything else on the screen.
    cost
    It's less impressive than a made-up rating.

    A broken invite link gets the same ticket as a real one, greyed out and stamped "VOID," instead of a generic error page.

03 · how the day gets built

Votes become the day

Start a trip, share the link, and everyone hearts the places they want to go. Fyzz turns the votes into a day that works with the weather, the opening hours and the time it takes to get between stops.

Each person taps a heart on the places they care about. The top pick gets a "Fyzzing" badge, and if two places tie, they both get it. There's no tiebreaker in v1, so it just shows the tie.

When you're ready, Fyzz builds the day from the votes. "Pack my day" fits in as much as it can. "Keep it loose" leaves room between stops and caps the day at about one thing every two hours. Reservations are fixed in the data itself, so nothing ever moves them automatically.

While using it, I noticed a place with a vote (Casa Loma, opens at 9:30) losing its spot to places nobody voted for. The schedule tried to start it at 9:00, couldn't, and skipped it. Now it waits for a place to open, the way a real day works.

A heads-up before the day goes sideways

Each stop gets checked against the hourly forecast. If rain's coming, a note at the top of the itinerary says when it starts and which stop it reaches, and Review offers a swap. The note and Review always point at the same stop, so you never read about one problem and get offered a fix for another.

Timing conflicts work the same way, like a stop running past a reservation's start time (the one shown here). A suggested change shows the before and after side by side, says why, and mentions that everyone else on the trip can see it too. Whoever responds first decides. There's no countdown and no pressure.

04 · design system + voice

Everything means something

I wanted everything in Fyzz to mean something. Each colour has one job, and the ticket look only shows up on stops that are actually scheduled. The system did drift while the build moved fast. By early November there were 23 text sizes, five greys and four font families, so I pulled it all back into one system.

One job per colour

  • Coral is the main colour, and it means "go look at something."
  • Teal only ever means add, create or edit.
  • Indigo is social: notes, requests, someone nudging you.
  • Blue is "happening now," like the you-are-here dot on a map. I tried red first and it got mixed up with warnings.
  • Grey means fine, nothing to flag. Navigation like "Open in maps" is neutral too, since it doesn't change the plan.

The ticket

Scheduled stops get die-cut, perforated edges, like they've been punched out of a sheet. Places you're still voting on are plain cards. Giving them a ticket would suggest more certainty than there is.

On the trip hub, the itinerary tile is a sealed ticket stub until there's something to see, and then the tear line breaks open. It shows the real state of the trip.

Type and shape

Bricolage Grotesque for headings, DM Sans for everything else, and nine named text styles. Nothing gets a hand-picked size.

Shape follows one rule, based on what something is. Anything you tap or that holds content is a rounded rectangle. Status labels you only read are pills. Icon buttons and avatars are circles.

Accessibility

  • Every control is a real button or link
  • Reorder has up and down arrows that work just as well as dragging
  • 44px tap targets for anything you reach for mid-trip
  • 16px fields, so iPhones don't zoom in when you tap one
  • Nothing animates when reduced motion is on
  • Contrast checked on every pairing. I caught real failures (the heart icon, the wordmark, some recap text at 4.0:1) and fixed each with a safer shade

Tried and dropped

  • A timeline line that faded rose to gold to plum like the sky. It's one neutral dashed line now
  • Teal or violet for "fine." They sat too close to green, so grey won
  • A floating pill for the bottom bar. A pill means status, so it read wrong as a button

Voice and tone

Fyzz is a playful friend with a bit of an edge, but it saves the jokes for places they can land, like empty states and confirmations. Weather and plan changes stay plain and calm. And there's no fake urgency anywhere, because pushy copy would work against the whole idea.

  • Someone wants a change

    generic"User requested a schedule change"

    Fyzz"Priya wants this earlier"

  • A loose day is full

    before"3 of 6 places probably won't fit" (an orange warning)

    after"Your 3 most-voted here make it, 3 others stay as suggestions" (neutral info)

    The setting was doing exactly what it's for. If the warning colour shows up for expected behaviour, people learn to ignore it, including when it matters.

  • Rain on the way

    before"Rain expected at 2pm"

    after"Rain expected at 1pm. Affects Toronto Islands ferry at 2pm. Should you replan outdoor stops?"

    2pm was the stop's start time, not when the rain began. Now it says both.

05 · what I changed

Who gets to change the plan

This is the decision I changed my mind on, and the one I learned the most from.

When a whole group edits a plan live, it can get chaotic, and I didn't want that. So in early November I made the host approve changes. If a guest wanted to reorder the day or remove a stop, they sent it as a request, and the host tapped a check or an X.

It kept things calm, but it made the host a bottleneck right when people are still figuring out what they want. Going back over it a week later, I realized the chaos was never about people editing. What actually bothers people is the plan they agreed to changing under them. So I redesigned it around that.

early November

The host approves

Every change from someone other than the host waits for the host. One request at a time per trip. Calm, but nothing moves while the host is busy.

mid November

Sign-off is the gate

Anyone edits a draft. Once everyone signs off, the plan's locked, and it only changes through a new version everyone signs off on again.

  1. step 1

    Draft

    Anyone can add, remove or move stops. The activity feed shows who did what, with Undo.

  2. step 2

    Sign-off

    The host asks everyone. That version is frozen, and each person answers "Looks good" or "Request changes."

  3. step 3

    Locked in

    Changing it opens a revision. The agreed plan stays put until everyone signs off on the new one.

On the trip day, timing can bend. Anyone can stay longer somewhere, skip a stop or change travel time without asking, because by then it's describing what's actually happening. Everyone's told, and there's Undo. Rain 40 minutes out can't wait for the whole group to agree, so on the trip day a weather swap skips sign-off. Fixed times still hold, and everyone's told what moved.

What it cost

  • A week of work, partly undone. Host approval had been live for about a week before I replaced it.
  • More work for the host. "Request changes" is a note, so the host makes the change by hand instead of accepting it with one tap. I picked that over a second set of rules for suggestions.
  • One person can stall everything. Locked in means everyone agreed, and silence never counts as a yes. So the host can tap "Go ahead without [name]," and that person shows as "didn't sign off," never as agreeing.
  • Undo might be too easy. Anyone can undo anyone's change in a draft. I'm not sure yet if that's too easy, so I'll decide after the first real group test.

06 · results + next

What I learned, and what's next

Fyzz was built from September 4 to November 16. So far it's only been tested by me and one other person.

What using it taught me

  • Back kept going home. Reaching for back kept hitting the wordmark, which went to My Trips. Now back is always in the header with where it goes next to it, and only the logo itself is a button.
  • Push looked on but wasn't. Settings said "push" even when the browser wasn't set up, so nothing could arrive. Now it checks the device and says so, with "Turn it on."
  • An old trip came back. Reusing a trip name quietly opened an old trip that had already ended. Now every new trip is its own, and if creating one fails, you stay on the form and see what went wrong.
  1. 1

    Look past the first answer

    Host approval solved the problem as I'd first described it. Sign-off solved the problem people actually have. I only got there by going back to why it felt chaotic.

  2. 2

    Write down the cost

    Every decision has its cost written next to it, so when I replaced host approval, I knew exactly what I was undoing and why.

  3. 3

    The truth matters more than looking impressive

    "No photo" or "coming soon" is less impressive than a made-up rating, but people can trust everything else on the screen.

Next up

  • A real group test. It decides the open questions: whether Undo is too easy, and whether "Go ahead without" and "Request changes" work for real groups.
  • Follow-up chats with the eight people from the survey who offered.
  • Crowd data. A quick "How long was the wait?" on the recap is the first step toward crowdsourced wait times.
  • Day two for weekend trips, something for budget, and the getting-there problems people mentioned: parking, traffic and washrooms.
